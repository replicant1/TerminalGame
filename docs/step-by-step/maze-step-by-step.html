Carving and braiding, one step at a time

The same eleven by eleven maze the illustration uses, built one decision at a time. Press Step to make the algorithm do exactly one thing and say what it did. The random numbers come from a Mersenne Twister seeded the way CPython seeds its own, so a seed here gives the maze Maze.generate(11, 11, seed=n) gives there — cell for cell. Seed 7 is the maze in the illustration.

Junctions, and what counts as being next to one. The walk never stands on an arbitrary cell. It stands on a junction — a cell whose row and column are both odd — and it moves two cells at a time, because the cell in between is the wall it opens on the way. So the four cells a junction can move to are two away rather than touching it: from 5,3 they are 3,3, 7,3, 5,1 and 5,5. This page says "two cells away" rather than "neighbour" on purpose, because Maze._neighbours in the code means the ordinary thing — the cells actually adjacent to one — and the carve never uses those.

A cell is named row, column — the numbers down the left of the arena and the numbers across the top. Both indexes count from zero, and both start at the border, which is why every junction the algorithm stands on has odd numbers for both. The two indexes turn red to mark whichever cell the narration is talking about.

Watch the dead-end count during each phase. The carve only ever creates them — every retreat leaves one behind, which is why a perfect maze is nearly all dead ends. Braiding then takes them to zero, and the loops it opens are what let a ghost with no map and no plan keep moving.

The wall cells the braid cuts off from the border become the islands, and an island holds no pills because nothing can ever walk on it. That is the third panel of the illustration, and it is not chosen by anything — it is whatever the openings happened to leave.
